Section 1

Why Arm Care Matters

Throwing is one of the most complex and physically demanding movements in sport. It requires precise coordination of the entire body to generate and transfer high levels of force in extremely short time frames. When preparation is insufficient, stress accumulates quickly - most often at the shoulder and elbow.

In efficient throwing, force is generated from the ground and transferred through the hips, trunk, and shoulder before being expressed through the arm. The arm functions primarily as a stress receiver and force transmitter, not a primary force generator. When movement quality, strength, or control is lacking elsewhere in the system, the arm is forced to compensate.

Legs - Hips - Core - Shoulder - Arm - Ball
Force transfers through the entire chain. Weakness anywhere puts the arm at risk.
Fatigue Is a Primary Risk Factor

Fatigue is one of the strongest predictors of injury in throwing athletes. It does not always look like exhaustion. Fatigue in a throwing athlete can be subtle: a slightly dropped elbow, a pitch that feels harder to locate, an arm that takes longer than usual to feel loose. These are warning signs that the system is operating under stress it can no longer effectively manage.

When fatigue accumulates, muscle activation timing becomes delayed, movement patterns become less efficient, and the joints absorb forces they were never designed to handle alone. The arm does not fail because of one bad outing. It fails because stress was repeatedly applied to a system that was not given adequate time to recover.

Feeling good is not clearance to do more. It is an opportunity to recover well.
Preparation Drives Performance

Arm care is not a warmup ritual. It is the work - and when treated as such, performance follows naturally. Athletes who prepare well throw with more efficiency, maintain better mechanics deeper into outings, and recover faster between sessions.

The focus of any throwing day is throwing. Arm care prepares the body for that demand - it does not add to it. Every throw before competition is a throw spent. Protect them.

Long-Term Athletic Development

The athletes who reach the highest levels of the game are almost never the ones who threw the most as teenagers. They are the ones who were developed intelligently, protected when it mattered, and given the time and structure to build a body capable of withstanding elite demands.

Multi-sport participation through high school is one of the most protective things a young athlete can do. Sport specialization before age 14 has been correlated with higher rates of overuse injury, earlier burnout, and shorter athletic careers.

Section 2

AAM Arm Care Standards

Core PhilosophyTrain intelligently and manage stress to support long-term performance and durability. Arm care is not about doing more - it is about doing the right things, consistently.
Standards in Practice
Preparation Before Intensity
Arm care prepares the body to throw efficiently. Preparation is most critical on hard days - not least critical.
Stress Is Managed, Not Avoided
The right amount of stress at the right time, followed by adequate recovery, is exactly how durable throwers are built.
Progressions Are Respected
Progression is earned when the body demonstrates it has recovered and adapted. Repeating a stage is never a setback.
Quality Over Quantity
Fifty focused, mechanically sound throws does more than one hundred throws without purpose. Every throw should have a reason.
Feedback Guides Adjustment
Soreness, stiffness, fatigue, and mechanical changes are all forms of information. Take them seriously.
Consistency Builds Durability
Durability is the product of showing up consistently, doing the right things in the right order, and compounding small adaptations over time.
Section 3

Red Flags and Athlete Responsibilities

We train to throw. We do not throw to train. Every decision around preparation, recovery, and workload should reflect that principle.
Stop ImmediatelyIf you experience any of the following, STOP and seek evaluation from an AAM provider or qualified medical professional.
Pain-Related Red Flags
Sharp, stabbing, or sudden pain
Pain that worsens during or after throwing
Pain that does not improve with rest
Pain that alters mechanics or movement patterns
Neurological Symptoms
Numbness or tingling in the arm, hand, or fingers
Burning sensations
Loss of coordination or grip strength
Range of Motion or Strength
Sudden or progressive loss of shoulder or elbow range of motion
Noticeable weakness not related to normal fatigue
Inability to fully straighten or bend the elbow
Swelling and Structural Concerns
Visible swelling around the elbow or shoulder
Unusual tightness or pressure that does not resolve
Audible popping or catching accompanied by pain
Unresolved Fatigue
Persistent arm heaviness
Loss of throwing feel or control over multiple sessions
Declining performance despite adequate rest
Athlete Responsibilities
Train with Awareness
Pay attention to how your arm feels during warm-ups, how it responds to intensity, and how it recovers afterward.
Respect Fatigue and Recovery
Feeling good after a session does not mean the arm is ready to go harder tomorrow. Recovery happens between sessions.
Communicate Symptoms Early
If something feels off - say so. Not after the game. Early communication prevents minor concerns from becoming structural problems.
Never Push Through Pain
When pain is present during throwing - not soreness the next morning, but pain during throwing - the session stops. No exceptions.

Programs

Daily Throwing Order

Structure matters. The order in which an athlete prepares to throw is not arbitrary - it reflects a deliberate progression from low-demand to high-demand activity. Skipping steps or rushing through preparation removes the protective effect that the sequence is designed to provide.
1
Soft Tissue Work
Foam rolling and soft tissue preparation. Goal: increase tissue extensibility, improve blood flow, and prepare the body for movement. Move slowly and pause on areas of restriction rather than rolling quickly over them. ~5-8 minutes
2
Mobility and Correctives
Targeted mobility and corrective exercises addressing the specific ranges of motion the throwing athlete needs most - thoracic spine rotation, shoulder mobility, hip flexibility, and individual movement restrictions. ~5-8 minutes
3
Dynamic Warm-Up
Elevates heart rate, increases body temperature, and transitions the athlete to ready for athletic movement. Reinforces hip rotation, trunk separation, deceleration, and weight transfer - the movement patterns most relevant to throwing. A proper dynamic warm-up leaves the athlete slightly warm and moving well before any throwing begins. ~8-10 minutes
4
Movement Pattern Prep
Bridges the gap between general warm-up and throwing-specific activity. Reinforces position-specific patterns - weight transfer, hip-to-shoulder separation, deceleration mechanics. Goal: make the first throw of the day feel like the tenth. ~3-5 minutes
5
J-Band Routine
Activates the specific muscle groups responsible for throwing - rotator cuff, scapular stabilizers, elbow flexors and extensors, forearm musculature. Volume is intentionally low. Goal is activation and priming - not fatigue. One set of eight reps is enough. ~5 minutes
6
Plyo Prep
When prescribed, serves as the final bridge between activation and live throwing. Plyometric throwing movements introduce high-velocity neuromuscular demands in a controlled context. Not every athlete will have this - it must be individually prescribed. Never self-directed without provider guidance. ~3-5 minutes when prescribed
7
Throwing Schedule
With preparation complete, execute the day's prescribed throwing exactly as written. Volume, distance, and intent are all prescribed for a reason. Deviating from the plan - adding throws, increasing intent, extending the session - undermines the structure the entire program is built on. As prescribed
Short Box

A short box is controlled work with a specific purpose - it is not an opportunity to throw hard. It is a dedicated opportunity to work on mechanical refinement, release point consistency, and pitch execution without the full physical demand of mound work.

The short box exists because mechanical work done at full intensity is often mechanical work that cannot be evaluated clearly. At 70-85% intent, the feedback loop opens - allowing the pitcher to feel movements, make adjustments, and repeat patterns in a lower-stress environment.

Foundation

Arm Care Principles

Pre-Throw Preparation

Baseball is a game rooted in routine. While routine can support confidence, preparation must still serve a purpose. Pre-throw preparation should be individualized to the athlete, address current needs rather than habits, and prepare the body to throw efficiently - not excessively.

Critical RuleStop throwing when you are warm. Between catch play, long toss, plyo ball work, and pre-game bullpens, athletes can easily accumulate 100-150 throws before competition begins. Those throws count. Every throw before competition is a throw spent. Protect them.

Preparation is most important on hard days - not least important. An athlete who skips preparation and goes straight to a bullpen is asking cold, unprepared tissue to absorb forces it is not ready for. The harder the session ahead, the more important it is to arrive prepared.

Long Toss

Long toss can be a valuable tool when it reinforces quality movement and is programmed with intention. When done correctly, it builds arm strength, reinforces extension through the release point, and develops the tissue tolerance required for high-velocity throwing.

When done incorrectly - at excessive distances, with poor mechanics, or in high volumes on top of other throwing - it accelerates fatigue and creates movement patterns that carry into competitive throwing.

For most athletes, long toss should not exceed 120-150 feet. Quality of movement always takes priority over distance. Throwing farther is not always throwing better. Throwing on a line matters - excessive launch angles create poor habits that transfer to the mound.
  • Build distance gradually - do not start at max distance
  • Crow hop mechanics on line throws - not max-effort pull-downs unless specifically prescribed
  • 120 feet is the standard ceiling for most high school athletes
  • 150 feet is the absolute ceiling - rarely necessary and only for advanced athletes
  • Long toss volume counts toward daily and weekly throw totals
  • Never use long toss as a substitute for a bullpen on a prescribed off day
Weighted Balls
Age RestrictionWeighted ball training is intended for athletes 16 years of age and older who have completed a full baseline assessment, are currently pain-free, have no history of elbow or shoulder injury within the past 12 months, and are under the supervision of a qualified professional.
Appropriate Candidates
  • Age 16 or older with near-mature growth plates
  • Pain-free for minimum 6 months
  • Passed full baseline movement screen
  • Adequate shoulder strength - ER:IR ratio at or above 0.75
  • Internal rotation within 10 degrees bilateral
  • Strong kinetic chain mechanics
  • Under supervision of qualified AT or sports medicine professional
Do Not Use If...
  • Currently experiencing elbow or shoulder pain
  • History of UCL injury, repair, or reconstruction
  • Active growth plate - typically under age 15-16
  • Less than 12 months post arm injury
  • Elbow extension deficit greater than 10 degrees
  • GIRD greater than 20 degrees compared to non-throwing arm
  • Inadequate kinetic chain strength or movement faults
Intent vs. Velocity

Intent refers to effort level - not velocity. This distinction matters because velocity is an outcome, not an input. An athlete who chases velocity during a recovery bullpen or a return-to-throw session is using the wrong target. The target is the effort level prescribed.

This is why percentage-based language is used throughout this program. When a week calls for 70% intent, the goal is to throw at 70% of maximum effort - not 70% of maximum velocity. For pitchers, a radar gun can be a useful reference point. If a pitcher's average fastball is 85 mph, 70% intent is roughly 59-60 mph. But the gun is a reference, not the goal.

Velocity should never be chased during any structured throwing program. An athlete who consistently throws harder than prescribed is not ahead of schedule - they are accumulating stress the program did not account for.
Intent LevelDescriptionWhen Used
40-50%Easy catch - arm loosening onlyWarm-up, early RTT steps, recovery days
50-70%Controlled effort - mechanics focusEarly mound progression, short box work
70-85%Building effort - strong but not maxMid-progression bullpens, long toss ceiling
85-95%Near-competitive effortLate progression bullpens, game warm-up
100%Full competitive intentGame outings, late mound progression only
Keep high days high and low days low. The gray zone - days that are neither true recovery nor true development - is where chronic stress accumulates. Avoid it.

Guidelines

Youth Arm Care

For Little League athletes and parents
We Train to Throw - Not Throw to Train. A strong arm starts with a strong body.
5 Youth Arm Care Rules
1. Pain Is NOT Normal
Soreness after activity can happen. Pain while throwing is different. If your child says their elbow hurts or arm feels dead - stop throwing immediately.
2. Fatigue Changes Everything
Tired athletes move differently. When mechanics break down, injury risk goes up. Signs include throwing slower, dropping elbow, arm feeling heavy.
3. More Throwing Is NOT Better
Kids do not improve by endlessly throwing. Smart training beats extra volume. Quality over quantity.
4. Recovery Is Part of Training
Drink water, get sleep, eat protein, take rest days. Recovery helps the arm rebuild. Healthy habits matter.
5. Speak Up Early
Small issues become big problems when ignored. Early evaluation equals faster return and fewer missed games. Speaking up is not weakness.
Parents: Your Role
Help your athlete listen to their body, be honest about pain, respect rest days, and communicate with coaches and providers.
Game Day ChecklistDid I sleep well? Did I eat? Did I warm up? Does my arm feel normal?

If any answer is NO - talk to an adult before throwing.
